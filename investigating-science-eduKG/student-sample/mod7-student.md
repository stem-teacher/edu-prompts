### Content Focus

The scientific process is the most powerful tool available for
generating knowledge about the world. It uses evidence and measurement
to find truth and highlight misinterpretations and misrepresentations.
Science as a human endeavour is subject to human failings, which can
contribute to fallacies, misinterpretations and, on occasion, fraud. For
this reason, scientific processes attempt to compensate for human
failings by questioning evidence, re-testing ideas, replicating results
and engaging with peer review in order to evaluate research.

Students investigate claims through conducting practical and
secondary-sourced investigations and evaluate these based on scientific
evidence. They explore examples of scientific claims made in the media
and investigate the benefits of peer review.

### Working Scientifically

In this module, students focus on selecting, processing, analysing and
evaluating primary and secondary data and information sources. Students
communicate scientific understanding and information about factual or
fallacious claims. Students should be provided with opportunities to
engage with all Working Scientifically skills throughout the course.

Claim: There is 40% chocolate in each of Arnott's choc chip cookies.

Context/Background information: Arnott's claims that there is 40%
chocolate in each choc chip cookie. The cookie is composed of chocolate
and the cookie dough. Prior to opening each packet of choc chip cookies,
there is a likely chance that crumbs or chocolate of the cookie already
fell off or broke apart such that the percentage of chocolate in the
cookie has been changed. However, the experiment is performed on
multiple packets of the same product such that the uncontrolled
variable's impact on the results can be minimised.

Aim: To identify if the choc chip cookie is 40% chocolate.

Hypothesis: If the proportion of the mass of chocolate to the mass of
the cookie is 40:60, there is 40% chocolate in the cookie.

Risk assessment:

Identify Rating Minimisation

Sharp tools may cut a person and cause bleeding. Medium Avoid holding
the sharp end towards people and pass tools by allowing people to hold
the handle instead of the blade. When someone is bleeding, apply bandage
on the bleeding area.

Glassware can fall off the table and shatter, causing a risk of cuts to
skin and bleeding, Medium Keep all glassware away from the edges of the
table. If glassware shatters, let the supervisor know and clean up the
glass without direct contact by using a dustpan. Wear in closed shoes

Variables:

Independent: the selected choc chip cookie

Dependent: the calculated percentage of chocolate in the cookie

Controlled: same technique of cutting the chocolate out, same spatula,
same mass scale, same 50mL beaker, same product, same brush

Equipment:

Spatula/chisel

Brush

Cookie (stick to one brand e.g. Arnott's)

A 50mL beaker

Mass scale

Method:

Set up the experiment as seen in the diagram above.

Weigh out the mass of the cookie as a whole.

  --------------- -----------------------------------------------------------
  Contradiction   a statement or depiction that is inconsistent and does not

The influence of sample election and sample size

Scientists can inadvertently distort or bias the results they obtain
from their data through the way they select the sample used in the
investigation.

Sampling error can be introduced by:

- Inappropriate sample selection

- Poor sample size

Both factors can lead to the increased chance of random effects

Sample bias

Sample bias -- tendency to under or over represent a particular group
within the sample population

- Determine validity and reliability

- Occurs when the scientist or researcher fails to consider whether the
  sample, they are examining is truly representative of the population
  they wish to investigate

- It occurs when the selected sample is not the true representation of
  the population

Voluntary response samples:

- Biased towards people from a specific demographic who are more willing
  to participate in surveys and experimental trials; e.g., middle class
  English first language speakers

- Not always possible to eliminate sample bias in an investigation

- Scientist can acknowledge the possible sources of sample bias when
  reporting results so those reading the report can make informed
  decisions with this in mind.

Sample size

- Determines reliability

- Problems occur when sample size is too small to eliminate random
  errors from swaying the results e.g. flipping coin should be 50/50

- The greater the sample size, the more reliable the results are likely
  to be:

  - Collecting results for large samples is extremely time consuming and
    often costly

  - Scientists predict which sample size will be large enough to produce
    reliable results while eliminating unnecessary sampling that would
    only confirm the data already obtained.

  - Determining optimum sample size depends on confidence level and
    confidence interval

Confidence level -- expressed as a percentage likelihood that a repeated
investigation on a random sample will produce a comparable result

Confidence interval -- a value, with an associated margin of error, that
indicates the range in which a random sample form the population is
likely to fall into for a given measure or treatment

Determining optimum sample size typically depends on confidence level
and confidence interval

Controlling for bias

In a scientific report, scientists carefully manage and control their
methods and research subjects as well to eliminate bias. They use
several techniques to reduce possible sources of error that result in
bias.

Control groups

Control group -- the group in an investigation that is used as benchmark
in comparison to a second that receives a prescribed treatment.

- It helps to determine whether a change in dependent variable is due to
  the change in the independent variable or is it due to random
  circumstances.

- It is the benchmark in comparison

- Enable a scientist to determine whether a particular treatment or
  process applied to a sample group actually has an effect or whether a
  change in a variable is the result of random circumstances.

- Researcher studies the differences between the two groups: 1.
  treatment variable 2. no treatment to determine effect of the
  treatment when compared to the normal conditions

- Used in an investigation that seeks to determine the efficacy

- E.g. 2 groups of petri dishes with colonies of the same bacterial
  disease growing on them and then applying antibiotics on one colony
  while leaving the control group petri dish untreated. After incubating
  both groups of petri dishes under the same conditions, comparing them
  would determine the efficacy of the antibiotic

Placebos

- A medicine or procedure that has no therapeutic effect

- Inert (inactive) or pharmacologically (the characteristics and the
  properties of a drug that make it medically effective) mild 'medicine
  'that had a positive impact on a patient's health and wellbeing

- Prescribing a medication could increase a patient's expectations of
  feeling better

- Subsequent physiological effects that helped the patient's overall
  health even though medicine was inert

- Commonly used in trials for medical treatments

- Impossible to make a person unaware the are participating in an
  experimental trial, they will have the tendency to expect some sort of
  effect. This effect can be overreported or sometimes can result on
  physiological changes even when no active treatment is given

- To account for this effect scientists give one group the true
  treatment and a second control group a placebo and compare the
  reported effects

- If the true treatment group measured above the false positives/'noise'
  of the control placebo group then the scientist can be reasonably
  confident that the treatment is producing the effects observed

Double-blind Trials

- An experimental technique in which neither the participants nor the
  researcher in contact with them are aware of who is receiving the
  treatment and who gets the control.

- When a researcher is conducting research on humans it can create bias
  when they are communicating because the researcher may have
  expectations about the investigation outcome

- Own expectations can bias results -- recording results reactions, body
  language could give away expectation to participant

- Therefore, double-blind trials must be conducted -- the administrator
  is unaware of the treatment a particular participant may receive. Gold
  standard in experimental research usually used in medical
  research![](media/image11.png){width="6.258432852143482in"
  height="2.8784722222222223in"}

- Can be used to conduct taste tests in the food industry by removing
  the labels from food or beverage product and having a research
  collaborator unaware of which product is which provide the research
  participants with food. This process helps determine which food
  product customers prefer based on taste alone

Bias in medicine

- Humans naturally tend to identify objects and people collectively
  rather than individually. E.g. different species of trees with
  different shapes as 'trees'

- Process of classifying is called stereotyping -- classifying based on
  superficial characteristics and generalising to all individuals that
  fit within the bounds of that classification.

  - This stereotyping poses problems for medical professionals who have
    to make recommendations for health conditions affecting a wide range
    of people.

  - Effect- racial minorities and women → delayed diagnosis and
    treatment → higher mortality rates despite relatively equal
    prevalence of a disease in both populations

[Reasons for bias in medicine]{.underline}

- Observer-expectancy effect: an effect in which an individual making a
  judgement on the presented data is biased by their preconceptions of
  the subject to be judged.

  - Can have a negative effect in medicine

  - E.g. Robert Rosenthal asked students to rate the performance of rats
    in a maze. There was no difference in the rats; however, before
    running the maze, the students were told one group of rats were
    smarter than the others. Later the students consistently rated the
    so-called smarter rats better than the other rats when in reality
    there was no noticeable difference between both groups

- Survivor selection: Research phenomenon where not all participants who
  start the study complete it. Incomplete data on participants is
  removed to maintain the integrity of study

  - In a random group of patients, those that survive longer are likely
    to receive trial treatment and have their treatment assessed.

  - Those that do not complete the treatment do not usually receive the
    full treatment and are not assessed

  - This can bias the outcomes and results in treatment being
    recommended for future patients, even though it has minimal
    effectiveness

Evidence-based analysis

- If two or more variables are correlated, it does not mean that one
  necessarily causes the other to happen

- Correlation does not mean causation

  - Correlation: connection between two or more things, or a measure of
    how things are related

  - Causation: where a change in one variable affects the other variable
    also referred to as cause and effect

The Hawthorne effect

- Investigations can influence the behaviour of their subjects in ways
  that are not anticipated

- Hence researchers must take care to control the conditions of the
  experiment

- 1984 researches from the National Research Council and Massachusetts
  Institute of Technology in the US were conducting experiments at
  Western Electric's factory in Cicero in the Hawthorne complex.

- Investigating the effect of light levels had on the productivity of
  workers and noted that productivity increased

- However, researches noticed that workers in the control group
  increased their productivity even through the light levels had not
  changed

- Initially researchers believed it was because the workers were
  conscious of being observed and changed their behaviour

- Later it was found that it was because researches supervising the
  workers were much more approachable and more inclined to listen to the
  workers that the managers

- Resulted in workers feeling more positive about their work making them
  more productive

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and heart disease

- Investigation 1976-1996 -- to identify factors that increased and
  decreased heart disease

- Observation- women taking HRT after menopause (natural decline of
  reproductive hormones) had lower incidence of heart disease

- Result- led doctors to prescribe HRT to many women after they reached
  menopause

- Further observational studies showed no reduction in heart disease for
  women taking HRT

- Further analysis of data for women who took HRT in investigative
  studies -- higher socioeconomic background, had education, had better
  diet and healthy lifestyle

The Mozart effect

- Investigation -- 1993: 36 college students who listened to Mozart
  performed better in IQ test

- Result- Mozart effect - listening to classical music can rewire the
  brain and hence can increase the IQ

- Further studies showed- it is temporary and is restricted to spatial
  reasoning types of tasks.

- Caution- findings from small sample size should be analysed very
  carefully for possible error and bias.

Conflict of Interest

- Science can be appropriated to further the interests of different
  social, political and economic groups due to conflict of interest

- It happens when two groups have incompatible objectives.

- If these two groups have unequal power, then one group can use their
  power for personal benefit.

- There are many industries that are well-established and have
  profitable practice, but scientific findings are advising that they
  should be stopped or modified.

- E.g. fishing industry advised that they are overfishing in a
  particular area and that if they continue fishing there will be not
  enough fish to restock the are and the industry will collapse.
  However, this has happened and the industry increased fishing to make
  as much short-term profit as possible before the collapse

- Industries continue a given practice despite the warning signs
  because:

  - It could be very expensive to change business model or might go out
    of business if they cannot adapt quickly

  - Corporate model demands to get maximum profit for shareholders. But
    funding of changing business model can reduce that dividend and
    probable reduction in share price.

- Hence Corporations:

  - suppress scientific research that conflicts with their practice

  - engage in campaigns to misrepresent the findings

  - create confusion in public to delay the effective implementation

- If scientists are working in the same industry in which the
  investigation is being conducted, then the findings can be skewed or
  biased as their employment depends on the results that are favourable
  to the profit of that company.
