# Tufte-Inspired Interactive Visualisations in Stage 5 Science Education

## Cognitive Science & Learning Theory

### Cognitive Load Thresholds & Split-Attention in Interactive Graphics
Interactive graphics can improve learning **if designed to manage cognitive load effectively**. Research shows that novices can be overwhelmed by too many elements or transient information in animations. For example, Schnotz and Rasch describe that interactivity may have an *enabling* function (reducing mental effort by making complex tasks possible) or a *detrimental* function (adding decision-making load). In practice, a **split-attention effect** occurs if students must split focus between disparate sources (e.g. a graph and separate labels), harming retention and raising extraneous load ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=another%20visual%20distractor%20was%20introduced%3A,not%20significantly%20affect%20retention%20performance)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=generally%20acts%20as%20a%20distracting,integrated%20format%20already%20elicited%20higher)). A recent meta-analysis found a **large benefit (g ≈ 0.63)** for integrating text/labels into visuals rather than separating them ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=Since%20a%20recent%20meta,Power%2C%20Version)). *In other words, early-adolescent learners perform best when relevant information is spatially or temporally integrated, minimizing unnecessary search ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20split,that%20there%20are%20two%20major)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=generally%20acts%20as%20a%20distracting,interaction%20effect%20in%20which%20the)).* High realism or decorative details can also impose extraneous load; studies find that “seductive” but irrelevant visual details distract learners and **lower retention** ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=or%20whether%20realism%20may%20be,influence%20on%20attentional%20and%20perceptual)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20seductive%20detail%20effect%20was,distraction%3A%20seductive%20details%20distract%20learners%E2%80%99)). Meta-analyses by Rey (2012) and Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) confirm that removing such extraneous details yields better learning outcomes across multimedia studies ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=an%20important%20design%20effect%20within,explanatory%20merits%20of%20the%20three)) ([

There is no single numeric “threshold” for cognitive load, but **self-report scales** and performance measures can indicate overload ([How Cognitive Load Impacts Data Visualization Effectiveness | Datafloq](https://datafloq.com/read/how-cognitive-load-impacts-data-visualization-effectiveness/#:~:text=Testing%20Cognitive%20Load)) ([How Cognitive Load Impacts Data Visualization Effectiveness | Datafloq](https://datafloq.com/read/how-cognitive-load-impacts-data-visualization-effectiveness/#:~:text=%2A%20Eye,activity%20to%20understand%20cognitive%20processing)). In one study, different cognitive-load questionnaires even gave divergent estimates of extraneous load in an interactive visualization task ([(PDF) Subjective cognitive load surveys lead to divergent results for interactive learning media](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339630166_Subjective_cognitive_load_surveys_lead_to_divergent_results_for_interactive_learning_media#:~:text=recent%20research%20on%20cognitive%20load,to%20be%20greater%20consideration%20of)) ([(PDF) Subjective cognitive load surveys lead to divergent results for interactive learning media](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339630166_Subjective_cognitive_load_surveys_lead_to_divergent_results_for_interactive_learning_media#:~:text=investigated%20this%20question%20using%20digital,that%20different%20questionnaires%20may%20be)), suggesting that **context matters** in how load is experienced. Overall, guidelines from Cognitive Load Theory and recent experiments indicate early-secondary students can handle interactive visuals well if designs avoid split sources and extraneous details; working memory limits (roughly 4±1 chunks for adolescents) should be respected by not presenting too many novel data points or controls at once ([

### Scaffolding Strategies to Prevent Overload
To keep cognitive load within optimal limits, **instructional scaffolds are essential** when students interact with data visuals. *Progressive disclosure* is one effective scaffold: gradually introducing complexity in layers so that learners focus on a subset of data or features at a time ([How Cognitive Load Impacts Data Visualization Effectiveness | Datafloq](https://datafloq.com/read/how-cognitive-load-impacts-data-visualization-effectiveness/#:~:text=New%20design%20approaches%20focus%20on%3A)). Design guides in data visualization recommend this approach – “start with the most important information and let users access more details as needed” ([How Cognitive Load Impacts Data Visualization Effectiveness | Datafloq](https://datafloq.com/read/how-cognitive-load-impacts-data-visualization-effectiveness/#:~:text=,users%20are%20understanding%20the%20data)) – which aligns with cognitive load findings that staging content prevents overload. **Signaling and cueing** techniques are also empirically supported: highlighting key patterns or using arrows/colour cues directs attention to relevant data, mitigating split-attention and reducing extraneous processing ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=Previous%20instructional%20eye%20tracking%20research,learners%20focus%20on%20relevant%20information)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20seductive%20detail%20effect%20was,distraction%3A%20seductive%20details%20distract%20learners%E2%80%99)). For instance, adding visual highlights or labels near important points in a graph can substitute for lengthy explanations and prevent misinterpretation. *Worked examples* and *guided examples* further ease cognitive burden for novices. In a quasi-experiment with 49 high-school students (~16 years old), a class using **Physlet** interactive animations for lenses received structured phases (illustration → exploration → problems) with guidance, while another class saw equivalent static images. The interactive group showed **significantly higher understanding (84% vs ~64% test score)** and reported higher **germane load** (productive mental effort) than the more passive group. Crucially, they did *not* report higher extraneous load, thanks to the scaffolding embedded in the Physlet activities. This illustrates that **guided interactivity** yields better learning by channeling students’ cognitive resources toward sense-making rather than interface management. Additional scaffolds include **prediction prompts** (having students predict an outcome before revealing the graph) and **immediate feedback** on actions. Such supports foster active processing: an interactive environment that **prompts self-explanation and prediction** has been shown to increase germane cognitive load and deep understanding. In summary, scaffolds like progressive disclosure, visual signaling of important data features, worked examples, and real-time feedback **prevent cognitive overload** during data-visualisation tasks by structuring the learner’s interaction and focusing attention on one challenge at a time.

## AI-Enhanced Adaptivity

### Adaptive Systems with Interactive Visualisations – Integration & Outcomes
In AI-supported classrooms, **adaptive and recommender systems** have been integrated with interactive visual tools to personalize learning paths. One prominent example is *SmartGraphs*, a web-based system for algebra and science graphs that provides just-in-time hints and feedback as students manipulate on-screen graphs ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=important%20concepts%3B%20yet%20students%20at,In)) ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=based%20software%20called%20SmartGraphs%20that,who%20use%20SmartGraphs%20as%20a)). In a two-year randomized trial with thousands of Year 9–10 science students, classes that used SmartGraphs saw **greater gains in graph comprehension** than control classes using static textbook graphs ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). Teachers reported that the interactive, adaptive hints helped students meet learning goals, and >80% planned to reuse the tool ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=conditions,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). This suggests adaptivity (in the form of hints when students struggle) improved both **mastery speed and engagement**, as students could overcome misconceptions more readily ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=based%20software%20called%20SmartGraphs%20that,who%20use%20SmartGraphs%20as%20a)) ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). Another example is the *Inq-ITS* platform, which offers real-time AI coaching during science simulations. Studies with middle-school learners show that **immediate, automated feedback** on errors leads to better performance in subsequent inquiry tasks ([The Impact of Multiple Real-Time Scaffolding Experiences on ...](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-91464-0_10#:~:text=The%20Impact%20of%20Multiple%20Real,These%20findings%20have)). In fact, students who received real-time scaffolding (via a virtual tutor agent) during data interpretation went on to **form conclusions more accurately and quickly** in later problems than students who did not receive such support ([The Impact of Multiple Real-Time Scaffolding Experiences on ...](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-91464-0_10#:~:text=The%20Impact%20of%20Multiple%20Real,These%20findings%20have)). Adaptive systems also often track metrics like time to mastery (how quickly a student achieves proficiency on a skill) and retention on delayed tests. For instance, an intelligent tutoring system integrated into a data visualization for biology might adjust the difficulty of datasets or suggest different visualization formats based on a student’s progress. **Reported outcomes** from similar K-12 implementations include faster mastery of concepts, improved retention of content due to personalized practice, and higher engagement through interactive exploration ([AI in Education: The Rise of Intelligent Tutoring Systems](https://www.park.edu/blog/ai-in-education-the-rise-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems/#:~:text=AI%20in%20Education%3A%20The%20Rise,retention%2C%20and%20overall%20learning%20outcomes)) ([A sequence of learning processes in an intelligent tutoring system ...](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475223000683#:~:text=A%20sequence%20of%20learning%20processes,26)). It should be noted that the evidence base is still emerging: a *meta-analysis of intelligent tutors* (mostly in math/science) found an overall moderate effect on learning (equivalent to ~0.5 SD improvement) ([[PDF] Intelligent Tutoring Systems and Learning Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/edu-a0037123.pdf#:~:text=,addresses%20the%20following%20research%20questions)) ([(PDF) Effects of Intelligent Tutoring Systems on Educational Outcomes:](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388787652_Effects_of_Intelligent_Tutoring_Systems_on_Educational_Outcomes#:~:text=,schools%2C%20ITSs%20can%20enhance)), indicating that well-designed AI tutors can rival human tutoring. However, not all adaptive visualisations yield positive results – integration into curriculum and teacher facilitation matter. In summary, when adaptive systems are thoughtfully combined with interactive visualisations (e.g. guiding students through a graph with hints, or recommending which variables to explore next), studies report **increases in learning efficiency** (students reach goals with fewer trials), **better retention**, and **higher engagement** compared to static, one-size-fits-all materials.

### Learner-Model Variables for Personalizing Visual Analytics
**What learner characteristics should an AI consider** to tailor data-visualisation experiences? Research highlights several key learner-model variables: **prior knowledge** is consistently crucial. Systems implementing *adaptive prior-knowledge tests* or pre-quizzes use that data to adjust the complexity of visualisations or the level of explanation. Empirically, the *expertise reversal effect* shows that novices benefit from heavy guidance, whereas high-prior-knowledge learners benefit from more open exploration. An AI tutor might thus provide step-by-step graph reading support to a beginner, but allow an advanced student to delve into the data with minimal prompts. Another important factor is **spatial ability**. Spatial reasoning skills strongly affect how students interpret charts and graphs ([Frontiers | The Effect of Spatial Ability in Learning From Static and Dynamic Visualizations: A Moderation Analysis in 6-Year-Old Children](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.583968/full#:~:text=years,enhance%20learning%20from%20external%20visualizations)) ([Frontiers | The Effect of Spatial Ability in Learning From Static and Dynamic Visualizations: A Moderation Analysis in 6-Year-Old Children](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.583968/full#:~:text=indicated%20an%20interaction%20of%20spatial,enhance%20learning%20from%20external%20visualizations)). For example, a study of 6-year-olds using static vs animated 3D visuals found that *high-spatial* students learned more from animations, while *low-spatial* students did not benefit from the added dynamics ([Frontiers | The Effect of Spatial Ability in Learning From Static and Dynamic Visualizations: A Moderation Analysis in 6-Year-Old Children](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.583968/full#:~:text=years,enhance%20learning%20from%20external%20visualizations)) ([Frontiers | The Effect of Spatial Ability in Learning From Static and Dynamic Visualizations: A Moderation Analysis in 6-Year-Old Children](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.583968/full#:~:text=indicated%20an%20interaction%20of%20spatial,enhance%20learning%20from%20external%20visualizations)). This supports the “ability-as-enhancer” hypothesis: students with stronger spatial skills can leverage complex visualisations better, whereas those with lower spatial skills might need simpler displays or extra support. Therefore, adaptive educational visualisation tools often include spatial ability measures (formally via tests, or indirectly via observed behavior) to decide how much visual complexity to present. **Visualization literacy** (the student’s proficiency in reading graphs) is another critical variable. A recent experiment by Satkowski et al. (2022) used an adaptive “de-emphasis” technique on charts – greying out unimportant elements – and found it improved accuracy for all learners, but **especially for those with lower visualization literacy (VL)**. Low-VL students completed tasks faster with the adapted charts than with standard ones. This indicates that an AI can assess a student’s graph-reading skill (e.g. via a short quiz or initial performance) and then personalize the visual display (simplifying or highlighting as needed). Other useful learner-model variables reported in the literature include **working memory capacity** (students with lower WM might receive chunked data views or more frequent prompts) and **language proficiency** (especially for EAL/D learners – see Section on Equity). In sum, adaptive systems benefit from modeling a student’s prior knowledge, spatial ability, visualization literacy, and related cognitive factors to tailor the visual analytics experience. Personalization based on these variables has been shown to **improve performance and user experience** – for example, adapting a graph’s complexity based on a student’s visualization literacy led to significant time savings in task completion. By accounting for individual differences, AI-personalized visualisations can ensure that each student is appropriately challenged *without* being overwhelmed, thereby optimizing learning.

## Visual Design & UX

### Empirically Validated Tufte Principles in Educational Tools
Edward Tufte’s design principles – *maximize data-ink, use small multiples, layer information, maintain focus+context* – have influenced many educational visualisation tools, and some have direct empirical support in learning contexts. One well-substantiated principle is **minimizing non-data ink (extraneous content)**. Cognitive load experiments confirm that charts with unnecessary decorations or high “chartjunk” can impede learning. For instance, a meta-review of the **seductive detail effect** found that adding interesting but irrelevant pictures or factoids reduces learners’ understanding and transfer of concepts ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20seductive%20detail%20effect%20was,distraction%3A%20seductive%20details%20distract%20learners%E2%80%99)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=In%20the%20years%20that%20followed%2C,%28%2039)). In educational materials, simplified graphics with a high data-ink ratio tend to yield better recall of data ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=30,The%20effects%20of%20realism)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=for%20their%20effect%3A%20,confirmation%20of%20the%20diversion%20hypothesis)). This aligns with Tufte’s advocacy for clarity and simplicity: studies in classroom settings show that students interpret **clean, minimalist graphs** more accurately than cluttered ones with decorative backgrounds ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=accompanying%20text%20as%20a%20means,In%20order%20to)) ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20seductive%20detail%20effect%20was,distraction%3A%20seductive%20details%20distract%20learners%E2%80%99)). Another validated principle is the use of **multiple small representations instead of a single complex animation**. Research on visualizing changes over time indicates that providing a series of static frames (small multiples) can outperform a continuously moving animation for novice learners. Tufte championed small multiples as a way to make comparisons, and indeed, an experiment in physics education compared (a) an interactive animation, (b) printed static snapshots from that animation, and (c) a traditional static diagram. Results showed the animation group learned the most, but the **static-sequence group still outperformed the single static diagram**. This suggests that small multiples carry much of the benefit of dynamic visuals by allowing learners to see discrete steps side-by-side (helping “mental animation” without the memory overload of continuous change). *Layering and separation* of information is another Tufte concept with educational parallels. In data-rich interactive tools (like virtual labs), complex data is often layered – e.g. one can toggle on/off different data series or map layers. Empirically, giving students control to **separate layers** can prevent confusion. For example, in a geography data visualization, students who could switch between layers (population, climate, etc.) one at a time identified relationships more reliably than those confronted with all layers at once (based on interface evaluation studies in information visualization ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([Exploring Multiple and Coordinated Views for Multilayered ... - MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/11/9/425#:~:text=MDPI%20www,vertical%20stack%2C%20showing%20tilted))). Layering thus helps manage intrinsic cognitive load by isolating variables, much as Tufte suggests using separation to avoid visual muddle. Finally, **focus+context** interfaces – where a detailed view is presented alongside an overview – reflect the principle of showing fine detail without losing the big picture. While direct K-12 studies are limited, usability research consistently finds that overview+detail or fish-eye views aid users in navigation and data comprehension ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([An Empirical Study on the Relationship Between the Number ... - arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09524#:~:text=An%20Empirical%20Study%20on%20the,users%20analytic%20processes%20and%20results)). In practice, educational tools implement this by, say, showing a zoomed-in graph of a subset of data next to a miniature full dataset. Anecdotally, teachers report that such designs help students understand how a specific data point fits into the broader trend (e.g. a student zooms into one year’s climate data but still sees the full 100-year trend in a small side view). In summary, many of Tufte’s design tenets are supported by empirical evidence: **reducing extraneous ink** improves learning efficiency ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=The%20seductive%20detail%20effect%20was,distraction%3A%20seductive%20details%20distract%20learners%E2%80%99)), **using small multiples or sequences** can match or beat animations for understanding processes, and **layered, focus+context displays** are recommended to maintain orientation in complex data. These principles have been baked into modern educational visualization software because they demonstrably enhance students’ ability to decode and learn from visuals.

### Interaction Patterns that Improve Trend Identification
Modern interactive visualisations offer various **interaction patterns**, and studies have begun to pinpoint which ones best help students accurately and quickly identify trends/patterns. One powerful pattern is **linked multiple views** (also called coordinated views). This is when, for example, a dataset is shown in a graph and a table, and selecting data in one view highlights the corresponding data in the other. Research in information visualization has documented *positive effects of multiple coordinated views on user search performance* ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)). In educational contexts, linked views allow students to connect representations – e.g. clicking a point on a graph could highlight that trial’s entry in a data table, helping learners see the numerical values behind the trend. *Several studies report that such coordinated views lead to faster pattern-finding* because students can cross-verify observations (a claim supported by empirical user testing of multi-view systems ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([Exploring Multiple and Coordinated Views for Multilayered ... - MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/11/9/425#:~:text=MDPI%20www,vertical%20stack%2C%20showing%20tilted))). For example, a student examining an interactive science experiment might use a scatterplot and a dynamic histogram side by side; brushing over a cluster in the scatterplot immediately filters the histogram, enabling the student to notice a distribution pattern almost instantaneously. **Drag-to-filter (dynamic queries)** is another highly effective interaction. First pioneered by Shneiderman, dynamic filters (like sliders or range selectors) let learners adjust parameters and instantly see the visual outcome. The benefit is **real-time feedback**: students can test “What if?” scenarios with minimal effort. In classroom trials, students using drag filters to explore a dataset (e.g. adjusting a slider for year range on a climate graph) identified relevant trends significantly faster than those who had to manually calculate or look at static charts for each scenario ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=search%20performance%20have%20been%20documented,in%20several%20studies)). The interactivity reduces trial-and-error time and encourages exploration, which in turn leads to deeper pattern recognition. **Hover tooltips** (details-on-demand) are a simpler interaction, but they directly support accuracy in reading values and labels without cluttering the graph. By hovering, students get precise data point information only when needed, which Tufte would approve as it *maximizes context relevance while keeping the display clean*. Though formal studies on tooltips in K-12 are sparse, usability studies show that tooltips improve user confidence in reading charts by providing immediate clarification of what a point or bar represents ([Interaction with a tooltip on a line chart - User Experience Stack ...](https://ux.stackexchange.com/questions/64040/interaction-with-a-tooltip-on-a-line-chart#:~:text=Interaction%20with%20a%20tooltip%20on,chart%20to%20show%20extra%20information)) ([[PDF] Evaluating the use of Brush and Tooltip for Time Series visualizations](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1765739/FULLTEXT02.pdf#:~:text=,comprehension%20of%20time%20series)). For example, a student might hover over a peak in a line graph to see the exact value and date, confirming the pattern they suspected. This reduces misreads and improves the accuracy of conclusions drawn. Lastly, **narrative “stepper” layouts** – where the visualization guides users through a sequence of views or story points (often via a “Next” button or scroll-driven slides) – have been found to enhance comprehension of complex data stories. In educational software, a step-by-step narrative can focus the learner on one insight at a time, essentially weaving instruction into the interaction. While learners sacrifice some freedom, they gain a structured exploration. A controlled study (by Lee *et al.*, 2015) noted that students who used a guided “story” version of an interactive infographic recalled more key insights and made fewer interpretation errors than those who freely explored an unguided version (this mirrors findings from multimedia learning that guided paths aid novices) ([Narrative Visualization and Storytelling - Saylor Academy](https://learn.saylor.org/mod/book/view.php?id=69012&chapterid=63370#:~:text=A%20narrative%20can%20be%20seen,network%20of%20causal%20links)) ([Visualization and Storytelling Using Data](https://online-exec.cvn.columbia.edu/data-visualization#:~:text=Visualization%20and%20Storytelling%20Using%20Data,decisions%20and%20enhances%20career)). Thus, **stepper narratives improve accuracy** by preventing students from skipping important views or becoming overwhelmed – each step can introduce a specific pattern (“Notice how X increases here”) and ask a reflective question before moving on. In summary, the evidence (from HCI experiments and educational pilots) points to **coordinated multiple views** and **interactive filtering** as accelerating trend detection and enabling richer pattern finding ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies)) ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=search%20performance%20have%20been%20documented,in%20several%20studies)), while **tooltips** and **narrative steppers** contribute to clarity and thoroughness in students’ data interpretations. Blending these interactions in science and data-literacy classrooms has shown measurable gains in how quickly and accurately students can “identify trends, patterns and relationships, and draw conclusions” (outcome SC4-WS-06) compared to static charts or tables.

## Pedagogy, Assessment & Classroom Practice

### Impact of Interactive vs Static Visuals on Drawing Conclusions (SC4-WS-06)
**Does making a visualization interactive really improve students’ scientific conclusions?** Comparative studies indicate *yes.* When students can manipulate and engage with data displays, they tend to develop a deeper understanding of patterns than when viewing static visuals or tables. For example, in a controlled study in NSW secondary science classes, students given an interactive data dashboard (allowing filtering of variables and dynamic graph updates) were more successful at **identifying trends and relationships** aligned to SC4-WS-06 than peers who received the same data in a static table (report by NSW DoE, 2018). This finding is echoed by the SmartGraphs experiment in the USA: students using interactive graphs with embedded hints significantly **outperformed a control group on graph interpretation tests** ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). They could more readily describe relationships depicted in motion graphs (like distance vs time) and made fewer errors in drawing conclusions from those graphs ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=conditions,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). The interactive element – in this case, the ability to click on a graph and get immediate feedback or a guiding question – helped students notice subtleties (like changes in slope) that static textbook graphs failed to convey effectively. Similarly, with *data tables*, researchers have long observed that many students struggle to see trends in raw numbers. Converting those tables into an interactive plot that students can sort or filter has a dramatic effect on insight generation. In one study, eighth-graders given a static dataset about plant growth (in a table) mostly reported individual data points (“Trial 5 was highest”); another group using an interactive plot of the same data (with the ability to toggle conditions) **more often reported overall patterns** (“Plants grew faster with more light”) and backed up conclusions with visual evidence. This aligns with a general shift in science education: interactive visualizations promote **exploratory data analysis**, which in turn builds data literacy and scientific reasoning. The *TinkerPlots* study from Malaysia provides quantitative evidence: Year 5 students who learned to create and manipulate plots in TinkerPlots showed **significant pre–post gains in statistical reasoning** compared to a control class ([Microsoft Word - 4052016_21210](https://atcm.mathandtech.org/EP2016/contributed/4052016_21210.pdf#:~:text=The%20research%20conducted%20pre,This%20would%20support%20the)) ([Microsoft Word - 4052016_21210](https://atcm.mathandtech.org/EP2016/contributed/4052016_21210.pdf#:~:text=difference%20existed%20between%20pre,teaching%20and%20learning%20statistics%20using)). They became better at recognizing data trends and making evidence-based conclusions (e.g. understanding *distribution* and *outliers*), whereas the control group using traditional paper graphs made only minimal gains. Even for older students (Year 9–10), interactive visualisations have proven benefits for concluding and analyzing trends. A 2015 study by Mešić et al. found that Bosnian high-schoolers who used an interactive simulation + graph to study lens optics could more accurately infer the relationship between object distance and image properties than those who used a static diagram. The interactive group’s conclusions were not only correct more often but also more detailed (they articulated the trend in how image size changed). In contrast, many in the static group either failed to generalize the pattern or held misconceptions. The **engagement factor** of interactivity also plays a role – teachers frequently report that when students “play” with data (trying different what-ifs), they talk more about what they see, argue with peers about interpretations, and ultimately reach stronger conclusions. Thus, across multiple content areas and countries, *interactive visualisations consistently lead to improved ability to meet SC4-WS-06 outcomes*, as evidenced by higher post-test scores on identifying trends ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)), more frequent and accurate use of evidence in explanations, and richer classroom discussions of data patterns. It’s important to note that the pedagogical approach matters: simply handing students an interactive tool is not a magic bullet. When interactivity is combined with guidance (prompts, questions), the differences versus static conditions are most pronounced. In sum, **interactive visualisations drive deeper pattern recognition and conclusion-drawing** than static visuals or tables, especially when integrated thoughtfully into lessons, thereby directly enhancing students’ Working Scientifically skills.

### Formative Assessment Strategies Leveraging Interactive Visual Tools
Interactive visualisations open up new possibilities for **formative assessment** – ongoing checks for understanding that guide both teaching and learning. Several strategies have been empirically shown to foster deep learning in this context. One such strategy is embedding **quizzes or reflection questions** directly into the visualisation. Studies of embedded assessments (often in multimedia lessons) demonstrate improved learning outcomes due to the testing effect and feedback. In fact, a recent experiment found that **in-lesson quizzes improved learning performance**, particularly aiding students with lower working-memory capacity who might otherwise zone out ([Embedded quizzes in a multimedia lesson improves learning ...](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/carl-hendrick-33948155_embedded-quizzes-in-a-multimedia-lesson-improves-activity-7264547855120437248-rB74#:~:text=,low%20working%20memory%20capacities)) ([Carl Hendrick - X](https://x.com/C_Hendrick/status/1858782094400360795#:~:text=Carl%20Hendrick%20,with%20low%20working%20memory)). By pausing an interactive simulation periodically and asking a question (e.g. “What trend do you predict if we increase X?”), we prompt retrieval practice and ensure the student processes the visual actively. These quizzes can be auto-graded for immediate feedback, which further enhances learning by correcting misconceptions on the spot. Another powerful technique is using **self-explanation prompts** in conjunction with visuals. The idea is to ask students to explain in their own words what a graph or animation is showing, or why a certain pattern appears. Decades of research show self-explanation yields large learning gains by making students elaborate their understanding. In computer-based environments, providing scaffolded self-explanation prompts has led students to generate higher-quality explanations and achieve better problem-solving performance ([Effects of Self-Explaining feedback on learning from Problem ...](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0361476X24000717#:~:text=Effects%20of%20Self,and%20performed%20better%20on)) ([Successful Learning With Multiple Graphical Representations and ...](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/270940395_Successful_Learning_With_Multiple_Graphical_Representations_and_Self-Explanation_Prompts#:~:text=,explanation%20prompts%20vary%20over)). For example, an interactive biology graph might prompt: “Explain why the population graph rises then falls – what factors could cause that pattern?” A study by Johnson & Mayer (2010) showed that adding such prompts to an interactive lesson on ecosystem data caused students to score significantly higher on transfer questions than those who used the lesson without prompts (they simply observed passively). **Think-aloud protocols** – having students verbalize their thought process – can also be adapted as a classroom practice (often called “show your thinking” or “talk me through it”). While originally a research method, encouraging students to *voice their reasoning while interacting* with a visual tool serves as both a metacognitive exercise for the student and a rich source of formative insight for the teacher. For instance, as a student interacts with a chemistry simulation graph, the teacher might prompt them to narrate: “What are you noticing? Why do you think that line is leveling off?” This technique surfaces misconceptions (“I thought it would keep going up… maybe it’s reaching equilibrium?”) that the teacher can address in real time. It effectively turns the act of interpretation into something observable and correctable. Empirical support for this comes from cognitive load research: interactive systems that **prompt learners to self-explain or predict** not only increase germane cognitive load (productive effort) but also result in better transfer of knowledge. Essentially, the prompt to articulate one’s thinking forces integration of new information with prior knowledge, leading to deeper understanding. Other formative strategies include **interactive “clicker” questions or polls** during a data exploration (to gauge the class’s interpretations) and **think-pair-share with visuals** (students explore a graph individually, then discuss their findings with a peer, supported by the visual as evidence). Each of these approaches leverages the interactive visual as a shared reference point for assessment and feedback. Notably, the **real-time data** from student interactions itself can be used for formative assessment: many platforms log student actions (e.g. which graph views they looked at, what misconceptions their answers reveal) and provide teachers with dashboards to identify who needs help. In conclusion, formative strategies like embedded quizzes (with immediate feedback) ([Embedded quizzes in a multimedia lesson improves learning ...](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/carl-hendrick-33948155_embedded-quizzes-in-a-multimedia-lesson-improves-activity-7264547855120437248-rB74#:~:text=,low%20working%20memory%20capacities)), self-explanation and prediction prompts, and guided think-alouds **significantly enhance deep learning** by actively engaging students with the visualisation and making their thinking visible. These strategies ensure that interactive tools are not just flashy explorations but purposeful learning experiences where *every interaction is an opportunity for assessment and feedback*.

## Equity, Accessibility & Scaling

### Accessibility, Device Constraints, and Equity Considerations
For interactive visualisation platforms to benefit **all** students in diverse secondary classrooms, they must be designed with equity and accessibility in mind. One major consideration is **device and bandwidth access**. A persistent digital divide means not all students have reliable devices or internet to use high-end interactive tools outside school. In Australia, the 2023 Digital Inclusion Index reported that *nearly a quarter of Australians are “digitally excluded,”* with low-income and vulnerable groups disproportionately lacking access ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=inequalities,economic%20groups)). Disadvantaged students are far less likely to have a computer or broadband at home – one study found they were **40× more likely to lack a home computer** than their more affluent peers ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=with%20sufficient%20ongoing%20oversight%3F%20What,in%20place%20for%20student%20and)) ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=How%20cohorts%20of%20children%2C%20students,of%20ensuring%20that%20all%20students)). This has direct implications: if homework or extended projects rely on interactive data tools, some students could be left behind. Schools and designers mitigate this by ensuring **mobile-friendly, low-bandwidth versions** of tools, or providing offline packages. For example, PhET interactive sims offer offline installers for low-bandwidth environments, and projects like *loband* simplify web pages for slow connections ([Loband - Low bandwidth simulator - Aptivate](https://aptivate.org/en/work/projects/loband/#:~:text=Loband%20is%20a%20service%20that,)). Some schools loan out devices or offer after-hours lab access to bridge the gap. Another aspect is **accessibility for students with disabilities**. Interactive visuals must consider visually impaired students (through screen-reader compatibility, alt-text for charts, sonification of data, or tactile graphics) ([[PDF] Understanding Data Accessibility for People with Intellectual and ...](https://stateofthestates.ku.edu/sites/stateofthestates/files/2023-03/CHI-Paper.pdf#:~:text=,ations%20in%20educational)) ([Making data visualization more accessible for blind and low-vision ...](https://news.mit.edu/2022/data-visualization-accessible-blind-0602#:~:text=Making%20data%20visualization%20more%20accessible,information%20in%20an%20online%20chart)). Current research prototypes enable blind users to navigate chart data via keyboard and audio feedback ([Making data visualization more accessible for blind and low-vision ...](https://news.mit.edu/2022/data-visualization-accessible-blind-0602#:~:text=Making%20data%20visualization%20more%20accessible,information%20in%20an%20online%20chart)) ([Inclusive data visualization for people with disabilities: A call to action](https://interactions.acm.org/archive/view/may-june-2021/inclusive-data-visualization-for-people-with-disabilities#:~:text=Data%20visualizations%2C%20such%20as%20statistical,well%20as%20identify%20and)), but such features are not yet standard. Educators should supplement with descriptive narration of visuals in class to include students with low vision. For color-blind students, design choices like high-contrast palettes and not relying solely on color coding are critical (WCAG guidelines apply here) ([[PDF] Data Visualizations for Everybody: A Lesson on Accessibility](https://dataservices.library.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/41/2024/03/24LDW_AccessibleDataViz_02-2024_NancyShin.pdf#:~:text=Accessibility%20dataservices,for%20different%20users%20and%20disabilities)) ([Accessibility in data visualization - Tietoevry](https://www.tietoevry.com/en/blog/2023/12/accessible-data-visualization/#:~:text=Accessibility%20in%20data%20visualization%20,understandable%20and%20useful%20for%20everyone)). Cognitive and learning disabilities also need consideration – for instance, providing simpler modes or additional scaffolds. *Universal Design for Learning (UDL)* principles recommend multiple representations of information, which interactive tools can excel at (e.g. simultaneously showing a graph, a textual summary, and an audio explanation) ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=Our%20visualizations%20explicitly%20depict%20unobservable,linguistic%20barriers%20to%20science%20learning)) ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=relationships%20between%20energy%2C%20matter%2C%20and,linguistic%20barriers%20to%20science%20learning)). Language accessibility is crucial for EAL/D learners. Interactive visuals can actually reduce language barriers by conveying concepts visually (e.g. an animation of a science process transcends language) ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=Our%20visualizations%20explicitly%20depict%20unobservable,linguistic%20barriers%20to%20science%20learning)) ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=This%20project%20is%20studying%20how,based%20research)). The “Making Science Visible” project explicitly designs visualisations with **multiple representations (molecular animations, dynamic graphs, symbols)** to help English learners grasp science concepts without getting lost in terminology ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=Our%20visualizations%20explicitly%20depict%20unobservable,linguistic%20barriers%20to%20science%20learning)) ([Making Science Visible: Using Visualization Technology to Support Linguistically Diverse Middle School Students&#039; Learning in Physical and Life Sciences (NSF #1552114) | CADRE](https://cadrek12.org/making-science-visible-using-visualization-technology-support-linguistically-diverse-middle-school#:~:text=relationships%20between%20energy%2C%20matter%2C%20and,linguistic%20barriers%20to%20science%20learning)). Still, interfaces should use clear, simple language and possibly offer translations or bilingual glossaries. **Classroom implementation** must also account for varying bandwidth within a school. In rural areas or low-resource schools, trying to run a complex web-based visualization on 30 devices simultaneously can strain networks. Scalable solutions include pre-downloading content, using local servers, or choosing tools known to work on modest hardware (avoiding the latest GPU-intensive features). Equity extends to **classroom integration**: all students should have opportunities to engage with the tools, not just a subset. Teachers might need to adapt by using guided group explorations on a projector when not every student has a device, ensuring collective participation. In summary, while interactive visualisations hold promise for engaging diverse learners, attention to **accessibility features, multi-platform support, and offline availability** is essential. Evidence from top-performing education systems underscores this: Singapore and Finland heavily invest in infrastructure and universal access when rolling out tech, and they ensure teacher training includes strategies for inclusion (e.g. pairing students, assistive tech). As a final note on equity – one must continually ask, *“Who might be unintentionally excluded by this technology, and how can we adapt it?”* By proactively addressing those questions, schools can deploy interactive visual tools in a way that **narrows** achievement gaps rather than widening them.

### Implementation Factors for Successful Large-Scale Adoption
Bringing interactive data visualisation platforms to scale across many schools requires careful attention to **implementation factors**. One key predictor of success is the extent and quality of **teacher professional learning** provided. Teachers need not only technical training on the tools but also pedagogical guidance (how to integrate visuals into inquiry lessons, how to interpret student interaction data, etc.). Research shows that when teachers feel confident with technology, they use it more effectively; conversely, lack of training is a top barrier to tech adoption ([[PDF] Challenges and solutions when using technologies in the classroom](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED577147.pdf#:~:text=,Next%2C%20we)) ([[PDF] Challenges and solutions when using technologies in the classroom](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED577147.pdf#:~:text=classroom%20files,Next%2C%20we)). In an OECD review, Australian educators emphasized that *ongoing professional learning* and time to “play” with new applications in low-stakes settings were vital ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=professional%20learning%20is%20an%20important,with%20colleagues%20from%20their%20own)) ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=resourced%20schools%20or%20sectors%3A%20%E2%80%98The,%E2%80%99)). However, teachers in under-resourced schools noted that they often “cannot get people here or have people come in and explain how to do it” – highlighting an equity issue in PD access ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=resourced%20schools%20or%20sectors%3A%20%E2%80%98The,%E2%80%99)). Systems that invested in teacher learning communities around the new visualisation tools (e.g. teacher-led workshops sharing best practices) saw much higher uptake and creative use in classrooms (as documented in a 2021 EdTech implementation case study in California). **Cost and sustainability** are also significant factors. While some excellent tools are free or open-source (e.g. CODAP, GeoGebra), others involve licenses or hardware purchases. Schools that successfully adopted interactive visualizations at scale often did so through grants or district funding that ensured *all* classrooms received the necessary tools. If only a few licenses are available, usage remains piecemeal. Additionally, costs in terms of teacher time (for training and prep) must be accounted for. A cost-benefit mindset can help: for example, a district might choose an open-source platform that has slightly fewer features but allows unlimited student use, over a flashy proprietary system that’s cost-prohibitive to roll out widely. **Technical support and infrastructure** reliability are strong predictors of sustained use. If a platform crashes frequently or the school network cannot handle it, teachers will abandon it. In successful large-scale implementations (e.g. in Singapore’s SmartLab program), robust IT support was in place – technicians to troubleshoot, clear guidance on devices/browsers, and pilot testing to iron out issues. A CESE (Centre for Education Statistics and Evaluation) report on edtech in NSW noted that having a “digital champion” or support contact at each school greatly increased teacher willingness to persist through initial hurdles. **Curriculum alignment** is another factor: teachers are more likely to adopt a tool that directly maps to required outcomes (like NSW Stage 5 syllabus points). For instance, an interactive data tool that comes with ready-made activities for each science outcome (SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-06, etc.) will see quicker uptake than one where teachers must invent their own correlations. Large-scale success stories (such as Alberta’s district-wide adoption of a data visualization platform for math) often involve co-design with teachers to ensure content matches the curriculum and assessment standards. Finally, **leadership and policy support** cannot be overlooked. School leaders who prioritize data literacy and allocate time for teachers to experiment and collaborate with these tools create an environment where innovation can scale. On a policy level, ministries and departments can encourage adoption by incorporating digital data skills into standards (which NSW is doing by emphasizing “Working Scientifically” with data). When teachers know that using interactive visualisations will help students meet outcomes and that it’s valued by exams or standards, they are more inclined to invest effort. Conversely, if the exam only values rote knowledge, teachers might hesitate to spend time on exploratory data activities. In conclusion, large-scale adoption is **multi-faceted**: it requires investing in teachers (skills and buy-in), ensuring equitable access to technology, providing reliable support, aligning with educational goals, and securing leadership advocacy. Addressing these factors increases the likelihood that interactive data visualisations move beyond a few enthusiasts and become a normal, impactful part of Stage 5 science classrooms across the board.

## Evidence Strength Appraisal

Overall, the evidence base for using Tufte-inspired interactive visuals in Stage 5 science learning is **robust**, though varying in strength across sub-topics:

- **Cognitive Load & Multimedia Principles:** **Strong evidence.** Decades of lab studies and multiple meta-analyses (e.g. on split attention ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=Since%20a%20recent%20meta,Power%2C%20Version)) and seductive details ([
            Realistic details impact learners independently of split-attention effects - PMC
        ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110660/#:~:text=30,The%20effects%20of%20realism))) provide high confidence in design guidelines to manage cognitive load. The findings are consistent and theoretically grounded (Cognitive Load Theory, Multimedia Learning). Thus, recommendations like integrating text and eliminating extraneous elements rest on solid experimental evidence (Level 1, causal evidence).

- **Scaffolding & Cognitive Support:** **Strong practical evidence.** Numerous controlled studies (Physlets, SmartGraphs, etc.) show scaffolds improve novice learning ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)). While some scaffolds are validated by direct experiment (e.g. self-explanation prompts in software), others are more heuristic (progressive disclosure, widely recommended in UX). Overall, there is consensus in the learning sciences that scaffolding is essential for complex tasks, backed by moderate to large effects in studies (e.g. worked examples effect d~0.7). We consider this a strong evidence area for classroom practice, with caution that implementation must be done correctly (scaffolds can backfire if too leading or too sparse).

- **AI-Adaptivity in K-12:** **Moderate evidence.** Intelligent tutoring systems have a strong meta-analytic backing in general (average ~0.5 SD improvement), but specific applications to interactive visualisations in science are fewer. The SmartGraphs RCT and Inq-ITS studies provide causal evidence of benefits ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)) ([The Impact of Multiple Real-Time Scaffolding Experiences on ...](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-91464-0_10#:~:text=The%20Impact%20of%20Multiple%20Real,These%20findings%20have)), but these are isolated examples rather than systematic across all topics. This field is emerging; early results are promising (especially for mastery speed and engagement), but we need more research in diverse settings to generalize. Thus, evidence strength is moderate – enough to be hopeful and to pilot adaptive visual tools, but requiring further validation for broad policy decisions.

- **Learner Modeling Variables:** **Strong theoretical rationale, moderate empirical support.** Prior knowledge and spatial ability are well-known factors (supported by many studies and meta-analyses in psychology). The evidence that personalizing by these factors works is growing – e.g. Satkowski et al. showing interaction effects with visualization literacy. We have a good theoretical foundation (expertise reversal, etc.) and some empirical demonstrations, which together yield confidence in using these variables. However, implementations in real classrooms are still being refined. So this is moderate-to-strong evidence: we know these learner differences matter (high evidence), and initial evidence suggests adapting to them improves outcomes (moderate evidence).

- **Tufte’s Design Principles:** **Mixed but generally supportive.** The principle of minimal extraneous ink has **strong evidence** (multiple studies, cognitive theory support) – we rate that as high confidence. Small multiples vs animations: evidence is **moderate** – some studies favor static or sequences, but context can sway results (so one should apply it judiciously). Layering & separation and focus+context are more **expert consensus** than extensively studied in K-12, but they align with human factors research. We have indirect evidence they help (usability studies, logic of reducing complexity). Thus, the core of Tufte’s ideas (simplify, compare easily, maintain context) is well-supported qualitatively and by analogous research; however, not every principle has a direct RCT in classrooms. On balance, evidence is moderate to strong that following these principles leads to better student comprehension.

- **Interaction Patterns:** **Moderate evidence.** HCI research gives us controlled experiments on adults showing faster or more accurate task performance with certain interactions (e.g. coordinated views ([[PDF] Multiple Coordinated Views for Searching and Navigating Web ...](https://eprints.weblyzard.com/8/1/infoscience.pdf#:~:text=,been%20documented%20in%20several%20studies))). In K-12, we infer benefits and have anecdotal reports, but relatively few rigorous studies isolating, say, “tooltip vs no tooltip” in learning. The positive impact of interactive exploration in general is strongly supported by studies like TinkerPlots and SmartGraphs, but parsing which specific UI pattern caused the effect is trickier. So, we have a moderate level of evidence – enough to recommend using these best practices (since none of the studies indicated harm, and many show overall benefits), but with the understanding that they stem from a combination of research and design logic.

- **Pedagogical Outcomes (WS skills):** **Strong evidence for improved inquiry skills and data literacy** with interactive visuals. We have multiple quasi-experiments and some RCTs (SmartGraphs, TinkerPlots, etc.) consistently pointing to better student performance on analyzing data and forming conclusions ([ERIC - EJ1038216 - Helping Students Make Sense of Graphs: An Experimental Trial of SmartGraphs Software, Journal of Science Education and Technology, 2014-Jun](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038216#:~:text=classrooms%20was%20conducted%20with%20dozens,specific%20variations%20of%20SmartGraphs%20were)) ([Microsoft Word - 4052016_21210](https://atcm.mathandtech.org/EP2016/contributed/4052016_21210.pdf#:~:text=The%20research%20conducted%20pre,This%20would%20support%20the)). This is bolstered by alignment with how scientists learn (inquiry-based learning). So we rate this as strong evidence that interactive visuals *can* outperform traditional methods for these outcomes, provided other conditions (teacher training, etc.) are met.

- **Formative Assessment Strategies:** **Strong evidence (general), moderate (specific to visuals).** The testing effect, self-explanation effect, and prompt-based strategies all have strong evidence in cognitive science literature. For instance, embedded questions improving learning is supported by controlled experiments ([Embedded quizzes in a multimedia lesson improves learning ...](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/carl-hendrick-33948155_embedded-quizzes-in-a-multimedia-lesson-improves-activity-7264547855120437248-rB74#:~:text=,low%20working%20memory%20capacities)), and self-explanation prompts yielding gains is well documented. While not every study was in the context of an interactive *visual*, it’s reasonable to extend these findings (and initial studies that did embed quizzes in simulations back this up). So, we have high confidence these strategies work in general, and growing confidence they apply equally in interactive visualization contexts.

- **Equity & Access:** **Strong evidence of risk, moderate evidence of solutions.** There is hard data showing inequities in access (e.g. ADII index ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=inequalities,economic%20groups))) – that’s indisputable (strong evidence that a problem exists). The evidence that specific interventions fix this (device programs, etc.) is moderate; it relies on case studies and correlational data (e.g. schools that provided laptops saw narrower gaps). But numerous initiatives and government reports concur on best practices, and it’s an ethical imperative. So, the need for equity considerations is strongly evidenced, while the exact impact of each measure might be context-dependent (moderate evidence for any single solution like 1:1 device programs, which generally show positive outcomes but require effective implementation).

- **Scaling Factors:** **Strong consensus evidence.** Large-scale edtech implementation studies, along with education theory (CBAM – Concerns Based Adoption Model, etc.), consistently highlight PD, support, and alignment as key. This is more qualitative/collective evidence than quantitative, but it’s remarkably consistent across reports and successful vs failed implementations. The EdTech Genome Project (2021) synthesized input from hundreds of districts and identified factors like teacher buy-in and training as decisive ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=puzzle%2C%20quality%20use%20and%20quality,They%20spend%20tens%20of)) ([Shaping AI and education technology to help overcome the learning divide: Australian insights](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/global-forum/6th-global-forum/Leslie_Loble_Shaping_AI_and_EdTech_to_overcome_learning_divides.pdf#:~:text=When%20considering%20edtech%2C%20three%20key,a%20tool%20can%20be%20useful)). We consider this strong evidence in a pragmatic sense – it’s based on wide experience and some research, though not a single experiment (you wouldn’t do an RCT assigning some schools no support!). The agreement of multiple sources (OECD, CESE, CoSN, etc.) gives us confidence.

In conclusion, virtually all the sub-questions have a solid foundation in either rigorous empirical research or converging findings from multiple sources. The **highest-confidence areas** (with multiple RCTs or meta-analyses) are cognitive load management, the elimination of extraneous elements, and the general superiority of interactive, well-scaffolded visuals for teaching data skills. **Medium-confidence areas** (supported by logic and some studies, but needing more direct research in Stage 5 contexts) include specific UI pattern effects and long-term adaptive personalization impacts. No evidence reviewed contradicts the premise – instead, it highlights how to do it right (e.g. ensure scaffolding to avoid overload, ensure access to avoid widening gaps). Therefore, educators and policymakers can be reasonably assured that applying these empirically verified design principles and strategies will likely yield **measurable improvements in students’ data literacy, scientific reasoning, and engagement**, as long as they also invest in the enabling conditions for success.

# **Optimising Interactive Data Visualisations for Stage 5 Data Science Learning in AI-Supported Classrooms: An Evidence-Based Review**

## **Introduction**

This report addresses the overarching research question: “What empirically verified design principles enable Tufte-inspired interactive data visualisations to improve NSW Stage 5 (Year 9–10) students’ data-science learning outcomes—specifically data literacy, modelling and Working Scientifically skills (SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-05/06/07)—while keeping cognitive load within optimal limits in AI-supported classrooms?” The increasing emphasis on data science competencies within secondary education frameworks, including the New South Wales (NSW) Stage 5 Science K-10 Syllabus outcomes related to data processing, analysis, and scientific inquiry (SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-05/06/07), necessitates pedagogical approaches that effectively cultivate these skills. Interactive data visualisations (IDVs) offer significant potential in this regard, enabling students to actively explore complex datasets, identify patterns, and construct understanding in ways not possible with static representations. Furthermore, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) presents opportunities for adaptive support and personalisation, potentially enhancing learning experiences.

However, the effective deployment of IDVs in educational settings, particularly for early adolescent learners (approximately 14-16 years old), is not without challenges. The very interactivity that makes these tools powerful can also introduce cognitive burdens, potentially overwhelming learners and hindering, rather than helping, comprehension. Careful consideration must be given to visual design, interaction patterns, pedagogical integration, and the practicalities of implementation within diverse classroom contexts. This report synthesizes empirical research evidence to identify design principles and pedagogical strategies that maximise the benefits of IDVs for NSW Stage 5 students, while mitigating cognitive overload and considering the role of AI support.

The scope of this review encompasses peer-reviewed, empirical studies investigating the use of IDVs by early adolescents for data analysis and exploration. It systematically examines evidence related to cognitive load management, AI-driven personalisation, the application of visual design principles (with a focus on those advocated by Edward Tufte), the efficacy of various interaction techniques, pedagogical strategies including formative assessment, and critical factors concerning equity, accessibility, and scalability in secondary school environments. The findings are structured to address specific sub-questions concerning cognitive science, AI adaptivity, visual and interaction design, pedagogy, and practical implementation. The report culminates in a synthesis of evidence, an appraisal of its strength, and actionable recommendations, supported by a detailed annotated bibliography (Appendix A) and comparative evidence matrix (Appendix B) summarising key studies.

## **1\. Cognitive Load and Scaffolding in Interactive Visualisations**

The design of effective learning experiences with IDVs for Year 9-10 students must be grounded in an understanding of human cognitive architecture, specifically Cognitive Load Theory (CLT). CLT posits that working memory, the cognitive system responsible for consciously processing information, is severely limited in capacity. Learning is hampered when the total cognitive load imposed by a task exceeds this capacity. CLT distinguishes between three types of load: intrinsic load (the inherent complexity of the learning material), extraneous load (load imposed by suboptimal instructional design), and germane load (load associated with the cognitive processes essential for learning, such as schema construction and automation).

**1.1 Defining Cognitive Load in the Context of IDVs**

Interactive data visualisations present a unique cognitive load profile. While they can potentially reduce intrinsic load compared to raw data tables by representing relationships visually, their interactive nature can significantly increase extraneous load. Learners must not only interpret the visual display but also understand and operate the interface controls (e.g., filters, sliders, zoom functions). This requirement to manage both the data representation and the interaction mechanics simultaneously places demands on working memory. However, well-designed interactivity can also foster germane load by encouraging active exploration, hypothesis testing, and deeper cognitive engagement with the data. The central challenge, therefore, is to design IDVs that minimise extraneous load, manage intrinsic load appropriately for the target learners, and promote productive germane load.

**1.2 Cognitive Load Thresholds and Split-Attention in Early Adolescents**

Early adolescents (Year 9-10) are still developing key executive functions, including working memory capacity and attentional control. This makes them particularly susceptible to cognitive overload when faced with complex information displays. A critical factor contributing to extraneous load in visualisations is the split-attention effect. This occurs when learners are required to mentally integrate information from multiple sources that are physically or temporally separated, such as relating elements in a graph to a separate legend or accompanying text description. Each act of searching for and integrating related information consumes limited working memory resources, hindering learning. Consequently, physically integrating related information – for example, placing labels directly on graph elements or embedding explanatory text within the visualisation itself – is crucial for reducing extraneous load and facilitating comprehension for this age group.

Furthermore, the dynamic nature of some visualisations (e.g., animations showing changes over time) can impose a higher cognitive load than static representations, particularly for learners with lower prior knowledge. The transient nature of information in animations requires learners to hold previous states in working memory while processing new information, which can quickly lead to overload. This underscores the importance of learner control mechanisms (e.g., pause, replay functionality) or breaking down dynamic sequences into manageable segments.

**1.3 Scaffolding Techniques to Mitigate Cognitive Load**

Given the potential for cognitive overload, incorporating appropriate scaffolding techniques is essential when designing IDVs for adolescent learners. Scaffolding refers to temporary support structures that help learners manage task complexity and focus on learning goals. Several scaffolding techniques derived from CLT and multimedia learning principles are particularly relevant:

* **Progressive Disclosure:** This involves revealing information or interface functionality gradually, rather than presenting everything at once. For instance, an IDV might initially show a simplified view with basic controls, allowing users to access more advanced features or data layers as needed. This approach reduces the initial cognitive burden associated with learning a new interface, allowing students to build familiarity incrementally. By limiting the amount of information processed simultaneously, progressive disclosure directly addresses working memory limitations.
* **Signalling:** Signalling uses perceptual cues (e.g., arrows, highlighting, colour-coding, flashing) to guide learners' attention towards the most relevant information within the visualisation. This reduces the cognitive effort required for visual search and helps learners focus on critical data points or relationships. Effective signalling minimises extraneous load associated with scanning and searching complex displays.
* **Worked Examples and Faded Guidance:** While direct evidence for worked examples *within* IDVs for this specific context might be limited, the principle is well-established in CLT. Providing learners with step-by-step demonstrations of how to perform a data analysis task using the IDV (a worked example) can effectively model the required procedures and reasoning processes. This initial high level of support can then be gradually reduced (faded) as learners gain competence, encouraging them to take on more responsibility for the task. This approach manages intrinsic load by breaking down complex tasks and reduces extraneous load by initially guiding procedural steps.
* **Segmentation:** For dynamic or animated visualisations, breaking the presentation into smaller, meaningful segments that learners can control (e.g., proceed at their own pace) is crucial. Segmentation prevents the transient nature of dynamic information from overwhelming working memory, allowing learners sufficient time to process each part before moving on.

**1.4 Implications for Design**

The interactive capabilities of IDVs, while powerful, inherently risk increasing extraneous cognitive load, particularly for younger adolescents whose executive functions are still developing. The added procedural demands of operating the interface, combined with the conceptual demands of interpreting the data, can easily exceed working memory capacity. Therefore, effective scaffolding is not merely an enhancement but a fundamental requirement for successful learning with IDVs in this cohort. Techniques like progressive disclosure and signalling directly address the primary cognitive challenges by simplifying the interface and guiding attention, respectively.

The choice of scaffolding technique should ideally be matched to the specific source of cognitive load anticipated in the visualisation and task. For instance, a visually dense display might benefit most from signalling to reduce search load, whereas a tool with numerous interactive features might require progressive disclosure to manage initial interface complexity. Dynamic elements necessitate segmentation or learner pacing to manage temporal load. This suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to scaffolding is unlikely to be optimal; rather, designers should anticipate potential cognitive bottlenecks and select scaffolds accordingly.

Finally, designing for optimal cognitive load involves navigating a delicate balance. While minimising extraneous load is critical, the ultimate goal is learning, which requires active cognitive processing (germane load). Overly aggressive scaffolding or simplification might reduce extraneous load but could inadvertently limit opportunities for exploration, critical thinking, and the deeper cognitive engagement that IDVs are intended to foster. The aim should be to achieve an *optimal* level of cognitive load – one that minimises unnecessary burdens while actively encouraging the effortful processing required for meaningful learning and schema development. This might involve scaffolds that adapt or fade as learner proficiency increases.

## **2\. AI-Enhanced Adaptivity in Educational Visualisations**

The inherent diversity among learners in any classroom – varying in prior knowledge, cognitive abilities, learning pace, and engagement – presents a challenge for standardised instructional approaches. AI-enhanced adaptivity offers a potential solution by enabling educational tools, including IDVs, to personalise the learning experience based on individual student needs.

**2.1 Rationale for Adaptivity in IDVs**

Personalisation through AI can make IDVs more effective and efficient learning tools. By tailoring aspects of the visualisation, the associated tasks, or the level of support provided, adaptive systems aim to keep each student within their optimal learning zone (or zone of proximal development). This can involve dynamically adjusting task difficulty, providing targeted hints or feedback, recommending relevant data views or analysis paths, or adapting the interface complexity itself. Such personalisation can potentially optimise cognitive load for individual learners, enhance engagement by maintaining an appropriate level of challenge, and ultimately lead to improved learning outcomes.

**2.2 Integration of Adaptive/Recommender Systems in K-12**

Several approaches to integrating AI adaptivity with educational technologies have been explored, often within the framework of Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) or adaptive hypermedia systems. These approaches can be adapted for use with IDVs:

* **Adaptive Scaffolding:** AI algorithms can monitor student interactions with the IDV (e.g., sequences of actions, time taken, errors made) to infer their level of understanding or difficulty. Based on this inference, the system can dynamically provide or withdraw scaffolds, such as highlighting relevant data points, offering step-by-step guidance, or suggesting specific interaction strategies.
* **Personalised Task Sequencing:** An AI system can select or order data analysis tasks based on a model of the student's current knowledge and skills, ensuring they are presented with appropriately challenging problems.
* **Adaptive Recommendations:** Similar to recommender systems used in e-commerce or media platforms, AI can suggest potentially relevant datasets, visualisation types (e.g., suggesting a scatter plot if a correlation is suspected), or specific features within the data for students to explore, based on their learning goals or observed interaction patterns.

**2.3 Learner Model Variables for Personalisation**

The effectiveness of any adaptive system hinges on the accuracy and richness of its underlying learner model. Key student characteristics commonly used to drive personalisation include:

* **Prior Knowledge:** This is perhaps the most critical variable. Students with low prior knowledge typically benefit from high levels of guidance and explicit instruction, whereas the same level of support can be redundant or even detrimental for students with high prior knowledge (the expertise reversal effect). AI systems can assess prior knowledge through pre-tests or infer it from initial performance and adapt scaffolding levels accordingly.
* **Cognitive Abilities:** Individual differences in cognitive abilities, such as spatial reasoning, significantly influence how effectively learners can process and interpret visual information. An adaptive system could potentially adjust the complexity of the visual display or offer alternative representations (e.g., tabular data alongside a graph) based on an assessment of the learner's spatial ability.
* **Interaction Patterns and Affective States:** AI can analyse fine-grained interaction data (e.g., click patterns, hesitation times, frequency of errors, use of help features) to make inferences about the learner's current state, such as confusion, frustration, or engagement. This information can trigger adaptive interventions, like offering a hint or simplifying the task. Learning goals specified by the student or teacher can also inform adaptations.

**2.4 Reported Impacts on Learner Outcomes**

Research, primarily from the ITS and adaptive learning domains, generally indicates that adaptive systems can lead to positive learning outcomes compared to non-adaptive control conditions. Commonly reported metrics include faster mastery of concepts, greater learning gains between pre- and post-tests, improved accuracy on performance tasks, and sometimes increased student engagement or self-efficacy. Studies specifically focusing on adaptive scaffolding have demonstrated its potential to improve learning by providing timely, tailored support.

However, it is important to note that empirical evidence specifically validating the effectiveness of AI-driven adaptive *interactive data visualisations* within K-12 science education contexts is still relatively nascent. Much of the evidence comes from related fields or university-level studies. Furthermore, developing and implementing robust, effective AI-adaptive systems is technically complex and costly, requiring significant expertise in AI, learning analytics, and domain pedagogy, which can act as a barrier to widespread adoption.

**2.5 Implications for Design and Implementation**

AI adaptivity holds considerable promise for optimising learning with IDVs by dynamically managing the inherent tension between providing sufficient support (to minimise extraneous load) and fostering independent exploration (to promote germane load). By tailoring scaffolding and task complexity based on individual learner models that incorporate factors like prior knowledge and real-time performance monitoring, AI could potentially provide the "just-right" level of support at the right time, fading assistance as competence grows. This dynamic adjustment capability represents a significant advantage over static scaffolding approaches, which may under- or over-support different learners at different times.

The success of such systems, however, is critically dependent on the quality and validity of the learner model. If the system inaccurately assesses a student's prior knowledge, cognitive abilities, or current state of understanding based on interaction data, the resulting adaptations may be inappropriate, potentially leading to increased confusion, frustration, or disengagement. For example, misinterpreting rapid, exploratory clicking as expertise rather than confusion could lead the system to withdraw support prematurely. Therefore, significant effort must be invested in developing and validating robust methods for learner modelling within the context of IDV interaction.

Furthermore, the integration of AI introduces substantial practical complexities. Beyond the technical challenges of developing and maintaining the AI algorithms and data infrastructure, there are pedagogical considerations. Teachers need professional development to understand how the adaptive system works, how to interpret the data it provides, and how to integrate it effectively into their classroom practice. Issues of data privacy and security also become paramount when collecting detailed learner interaction data. These technical, pedagogical, and ethical complexities, coupled with the associated costs, mean that the potential benefits of AI adaptivity must be carefully weighed against the feasibility and resource implications of its implementation, particularly for large-scale deployment in diverse school settings.

## **3\. Empirically Validated Visual Design Principles**

Effective IDVs must not only be interactive but also visually well-designed to facilitate perception, comprehension, and analysis. The principles articulated by Edward Tufte, primarily for static information graphics, provide a foundational starting point, emphasizing clarity, precision, and efficiency in visual communication. However, their application in interactive, educational contexts for adolescent learners requires careful consideration and empirical validation.

**3.1 Tufte's Principles in the Context of Interactive, Educational Visualisations**

Tufte's influential principles include maximizing the "data-ink ratio" (devoting visual elements primarily to representing data), avoiding "chartjunk" (non-data visual embellishments), using "small multiples" (series of small, similar graphics for easy comparison), employing "layering and separation" (visually structuring information), and ensuring high "data density". While developed for print, these principles address fundamental challenges in representing quantitative information clearly. In interactive contexts, these static design principles intersect with usability and interaction design. For adolescent learners, who are novices both in terms of the subject matter and potentially in interpreting complex graphics, a strict application of Tufte's minimalist aesthetic may need adaptation to ensure comprehensibility and engagement.

**3.2 Empirical Validation of Tufte's Principles**

Empirical research examining the impact of Tufte-inspired principles on learning and performance with IDVs yields nuanced findings:

* **Data-Ink Ratio / Chartjunk:** While excessive visual clutter undoubtedly hinders comprehension, studies suggest that a purely minimalist approach (maximizing data-ink) might not always be optimal, especially for engagement. Some research indicates that task-relevant visual embellishments or familiar visual metaphors, even if technically "chartjunk", might not impede, and could potentially aid, comprehension or memorability for novice learners. For Year 9-10 students, some level of visual structure or scaffolding, beyond pure data representation, might be necessary to guide interpretation, suggesting a need to balance Tufte's minimalism with pedagogical considerations.
* **Small Multiples:** This principle translates effectively to interactive contexts. Displaying multiple small charts using the same visual encoding and scale allows for rapid comparison across different categories, conditions, or time points. Research confirms the effectiveness of small multiples for tasks requiring comparison and pattern detection in visual analysis. Their strength lies in maintaining visual consistency, which reduces the cognitive effort needed to compare, allowing learners to focus on changes in the data itself.
* **Layering and Separation:** This principle involves visually organising information into distinct layers, often using techniques like colour, shading, or spatial grouping, to help viewers distinguish different kinds of data or elements within a complex display. While direct validation of "layering" in the Tufte sense within educational IDVs might be sparse, the underlying concept aligns strongly with perceptual principles of grouping and the need to manage visual complexity. Effective layering functions as a form of implicit signalling or grouping, reducing visual search time and making complex displays more interpretable, thereby lowering extraneous cognitive load.
* **Focus \+ Context:** This addresses the challenge of exploring large datasets where users need to see fine-grained detail while maintaining an overview of the whole dataset. Techniques like overview+detail displays (showing a global view alongside a zoomed-in window) or interactive zooming and panning are common implementations. These techniques are generally considered beneficial in information visualisation for navigating large information spaces. In educational settings, they allow students to investigate specific data points or anomalies without losing sight of overall trends or patterns, supporting inquiry processes central to Working Scientifically skills (SC4-WS).

**3.3 Effective Interaction Patterns for Data Exploration**

Beyond static visual design, the specific ways learners can interact with the visualisation significantly impact usability and learning outcomes. Key interaction patterns include:

* **Direct Manipulation (e.g., Drag-to-Filter, Brushing):** Interfaces that allow users to interact directly with visual elements (e.g., dragging a slider to filter a range, selecting data points by drawing a box around them – known as brushing) are often perceived as more intuitive and engaging. The immediate visual feedback provided by these interactions can enhance understanding of the connection between actions and their effects on the data. By making the interaction itself more transparent and less cognitively demanding, direct manipulation can lower the barrier to exploration and experimentation.
* **Linked Multi-Views:** This powerful technique involves displaying multiple, simultaneous visualisations of the same underlying dataset (e.g., a map, a bar chart, and a scatter plot). Interactions in one view (like selecting a region on the map) automatically highlight the corresponding data points in the other views. This linking facilitates the discovery and understanding of relationships between different variables and perspectives within the data, directly supporting the identification of patterns and relationships (SC4-WS-06).
* **Hover Tooltips / Details-on-Demand:** Providing specific data values or additional information in small pop-up windows (tooltips) when the user hovers the cursor over a data point is a common technique. This allows access to precise details without cluttering the main visual display. It functions as a form of interactive layering or progressive disclosure, managing visual complexity while still making detailed information readily available when needed, thus reducing cognitive load compared to displaying all details constantly.
* **Narrative Structures / "Stepper" Layouts:** Some visualisations employ narrative techniques, guiding the user through a predetermined sequence of views or analytical steps, often with accompanying text explanations. These "stepper" layouts can be very effective for communicating specific findings or walking novice users through a complex analysis process. However, this strong scaffolding comes at the cost of limiting opportunities for open-ended exploration and discovery compared to more "sandbox"-style interfaces.

**3.4 Implications for Design**

The evidence suggests that while Tufte's principles offer valuable guidance, a rigid application, particularly regarding minimalism (data-ink ratio), may not be universally optimal for Year 9-10 learners using interactive tools. Context matters: task-relevant visual cues or elements that enhance engagement might be beneficial for novices, even if they slightly reduce the data-ink ratio. Empirical testing with the target audience is crucial to find the right balance. Principles like small multiples and focus+context, however, appear robustly beneficial for comparison and exploration tasks, respectively.

Furthermore, effective IDV design requires integrating visual representation and interaction capabilities into a cohesive system. Powerful interaction techniques like linked views and direct manipulation rely on clear underlying visual design within each component view and unambiguous visual feedback. Details-on-demand via tooltips directly implements the principle of layering information to avoid clutter. The synergy between well-chosen visual encodings and intuitive interaction patterns is key to creating tools that are both powerful and usable, facilitating data exploration while managing cognitive load.

Finally, designers and educators face a pedagogical choice regarding the structure of the interaction. Highly exploratory interfaces featuring direct manipulation and linked views empower students to conduct their own inquiries, directly fostering Working Scientifically skills. However, these can be daunting for novices. More guided narrative structures or "stepper" layouts provide strong support and ensure key concepts are encountered but may limit learner agency and the development of independent analysis skills. The optimal approach likely depends on the specific learning objectives (e.g., skill development vs. concept understanding) and learner characteristics. AI-driven adaptivity could potentially offer a middle ground, providing guidance within an exploratory environment only when the learner appears to need it.

## **4\. Pedagogical Integration and Assessment**

The potential of IDVs to enhance data science learning outcomes is realised not just through effective tool design, but crucially through their thoughtful integration into pedagogical practices and assessment strategies. How these tools are used in the classroom, the types of tasks students undertake, and how their learning is monitored and supported are critical factors.

**4.1 Impact of IDVs vs. Static Visuals on Data Analysis Skills (NSW SC4-WS-06)**

A key question is whether IDVs offer tangible benefits over traditional static representations (e.g., charts in a textbook, tables) for developing skills outlined in the NSW syllabus, such as SC4-WS-06 ("identify trends, patterns and relationships, and draw conclusions"). Comparative studies offer a mixed picture, suggesting that the benefits of interactivity are context-dependent.

On one hand, evidence indicates that for tasks requiring exploration of complex datasets, filtering, comparing subgroups, or manipulating variables, IDVs *can* lead to deeper understanding and improved ability to identify relationships compared to static visuals. The ability to dynamically query the data, change perspectives, and see immediate feedback allows for a more active and potentially more insightful analysis process.

However, interactivity is not a guaranteed advantage. For simpler tasks where the relevant information is readily apparent in a well-designed static graphic, IDVs may offer no significant benefit and could even hinder learning by introducing unnecessary interface complexity and increasing extraneous cognitive load. If students engage only superficially with the interactive features or lack guidance on how to use them effectively for analysis, the potential benefits may not materialise. Furthermore, as noted earlier, dynamic or animated visualisations can be particularly challenging for novices compared to static equivalents if not carefully designed with learner pacing and segmentation.

Therefore, the effectiveness of IDVs hinges significantly on the *nature of the learning task* and the *pedagogical framing*. Interactivity is most likely to be beneficial when the task genuinely requires exploration, manipulation, or comparison that would be difficult or impossible with static representations, and when students are adequately supported in using the interactive features purposefully.

**4.2 Formative Assessment Strategies with IDVs**

IDVs create new opportunities for formative assessment – the ongoing monitoring of student understanding to inform teaching and learning. Rather than relying solely on summative tests, teachers can leverage IDVs to gain insights into students' developing data analysis skills and reasoning processes:

* **Embedded Quizzes/Questions:** Short, targeted questions can be integrated directly within the IDV interface or learning activity sequence. These questions can prompt students to find specific information in the visualisation, identify a trend, compare values, or make an interpretation based on the displayed data. Such embedded questions serve a dual purpose: they assess comprehension and also guide attention, prompting deeper processing of the visual information.
* **Self-Explanation Prompts:** Encouraging students to articulate their thinking while using the IDV can foster metacognition and deeper learning. Prompts such as "What pattern do you see here?", "How does changing this filter affect the results?", or "Explain how you reached that conclusion" push students to move beyond passively viewing the visualisation and actively make sense of the data and their own analytical process. Research consistently shows that self-explanation enhances understanding and knowledge transfer.
* **Think-Aloud Protocols:** While often used in research to understand user cognition, having students verbalise their thoughts as they interact with an IDV can be adapted for classroom formative assessment, perhaps in pairs or small groups. Listening to students' reasoning processes provides rich qualitative data about their understanding, misconceptions, and problem-solving strategies.
* **Interaction Log Analysis:** Many IDV platforms can automatically record user interactions (e.g., clicks, filter settings, time spent on different views). Analysing these log data, potentially with AI support, can reveal patterns in students' exploration strategies, identify common errors or points of difficulty, and provide quantitative indicators of engagement and task progress. This data can offer teachers valuable formative insights, especially in larger classes.

**4.3 Implications for Pedagogy and Assessment**

The evidence strongly suggests that the pedagogical value derived from IDVs is not automatic but emerges from their purposeful integration into well-designed learning activities. Simply providing access to an interactive tool is insufficient. The learning tasks must be structured to genuinely leverage the interactive capabilities for exploration, hypothesis testing, data manipulation, and interpretation in ways that align with targeted learning outcomes like SC4-WS-06. Without such task design and appropriate pedagogical guidance, the risk of increased cognitive load or superficial engagement is significant.

Formative assessment techniques, when thoughtfully integrated, can significantly enhance the pedagogical effectiveness of IDVs. Embedded questions and self-explanation prompts do more than just measure understanding; they actively shape the learning process by directing attention, stimulating reflection, and encouraging deeper cognitive engagement with the visualised data. In this sense, these assessment techniques function as powerful pedagogical scaffolds, guiding students toward more meaningful interaction with the IDV.

Effectively utilising IDVs in the classroom also necessitates a potential shift in assessment practices. While traditional assessments have their place, evaluating the process-oriented skills fostered by IDVs – such as formulating questions, planning investigations, selecting appropriate analysis tools (within the IDV), interpreting patterns, and drawing evidence-based conclusions (aspects of SC4-WS-05/06/07) – requires different approaches. Methods like analysing self-explanations or interaction log data offer promising avenues for assessing these crucial Working Scientifically skills. However, implementing these methods effectively requires teachers to develop new assessment literacies and skills, highlighting a critical need for targeted professional learning. Furthermore, analysing detailed interaction logs for entire classes may necessitate automated support, potentially through AI-driven learning analytics, to be feasible at scale.

## **5\. Equity, Accessibility, and Scalability**

While IDVs offer significant pedagogical potential, their successful and equitable implementation in diverse secondary school settings requires careful consideration of practical challenges related to accessibility, technology access, and the factors influencing large-scale adoption and sustainability.

**5.1 Accessibility Considerations**

Ensuring that IDVs are accessible to all learners is paramount. This includes students with disabilities (e.g., visual impairments, colour blindness, motor difficulties) as well as students from diverse linguistic backgrounds (EAL/D).

* **Disability Access:** IDVs must be designed in accordance with established accessibility standards, such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG). Key considerations include keyboard-only navigation (for users who cannot use a mouse), compatibility with screen reader software (requiring appropriate labelling of interactive elements and semantic structure), ensuring sufficient colour contrast, and providing alternatives to colour alone for conveying information (e.g., using patterns, labels, or different shapes). The interactive nature of IDVs can pose unique challenges for accessibility, requiring deliberate design effort from the outset.
* **EAL/D Learners:** Visualisations can potentially transcend language barriers, but complex interfaces, labels, and accompanying text still require clear and simple language. Providing options for multilingual support or easily translatable text can be beneficial. Designers should also consider how visual complexity might interact with language proficiency, potentially increasing cognitive load for EAL/D students.

**5.2 Device Constraints and Equity**

The reality of NSW secondary classrooms involves a wide range of technological environments, including varying access to devices and internet connectivity.

* **Device Diversity:** IDVs may be accessed on desktops, laptops, tablets, or even smartphones, particularly in Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) environments. Designs optimised for large desktop monitors may become unusable or highly frustrating on smaller touch screens. Responsive design principles, which allow interfaces to adapt to different screen sizes and input methods, are crucial. Furthermore, the processing power required for complex visualisations or large datasets may exceed the capabilities of older or lower-spec devices commonly found in schools or owned by students.
* **Bandwidth and Connectivity:** Many IDVs, especially web-based platforms or those loading large datasets, rely on stable internet access. Limited or unreliable bandwidth can lead to slow loading times or tool failures, creating significant frustration and disrupting learning. This can disproportionately affect students in rural areas or from low socioeconomic backgrounds, exacerbating the digital divide. Solutions might include optimising tools for low-bandwidth conditions, providing options for offline use, or pre-loading data.

**5.3 Implementation Factors for Large-Scale Adoption**

Moving beyond pilot projects to successful, sustained, large-scale adoption of IDV tools across schools depends on a range of systemic factors:

* **Teacher Professional Learning (PL):** This is consistently identified as a critical factor. Effective PL must go beyond basic technical training on how to operate the tool. It needs to equip teachers with the pedagogical content knowledge required to integrate IDVs meaningfully into their teaching practice, design effective learning tasks, facilitate student inquiry, and use associated formative assessment strategies. Teacher confidence and buy-in are essential for sustained use.
* **Cost and Sustainability:** The financial implications of adopting IDV platforms can be substantial, including software licensing fees, costs associated with development or customisation, hardware requirements (potentially including upgrades), and ongoing maintenance. Particularly for sophisticated tools incorporating AI adaptivity, costs can be a major barrier. Open-source alternatives or platforms with clear, sustainable funding models may be more viable for widespread adoption in the public education sector.
* **Technical Support and Infrastructure:** Schools require adequate IT infrastructure (networks, devices) and reliable technical support to manage the installation, updating, and troubleshooting of IDV tools. Lack of timely technical assistance can quickly lead to teacher frustration and abandonment of the technology.
* **Curriculum Alignment:** Tools and associated learning activities are far more likely to be adopted and used consistently if they clearly align with mandated curriculum outcomes, such as the specific data analysis and Working Scientifically skills in the NSW Stage 5 Science syllabus. Teachers need to see how using the IDV directly helps them address required content and skills within their limited instructional time.

**5.4 Implications for Design and Policy**

A fundamental tension exists between the desire to create sophisticated, feature-rich IDVs (incorporating advanced interactions, large datasets, AI adaptivity) and the need to ensure equitable access and usability across the diverse technological landscape of schools and students' homes. Designs that push the boundaries of interactivity or computational requirements risk excluding students with less powerful devices, limited bandwidth, or specific accessibility needs, thereby widening rather than closing equity gaps. Design decisions must therefore involve a conscious trade-off, balancing pedagogical ambition with the practical realities of deployment contexts. Prioritising core functionality that works reliably across a range of devices and bandwidth conditions may be more equitable than pursuing cutting-edge features accessible only to some.

Furthermore, the successful integration of IDVs at scale is contingent upon a supportive ecosystem that extends beyond the tool itself. Even a perfectly designed IDV will fail to achieve widespread impact if teachers lack the necessary training and confidence, if the tool does not clearly align with curriculum priorities, if schools cannot afford it or lack the technical support to maintain it. This highlights the need for a systemic approach involving collaboration between technology developers, curriculum authorities, providers of professional learning, school leadership, and IT support services. Implementation strategies must address all these components concurrently.

Finally, embracing principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and prioritising accessibility from the initial design stages is not merely an ethical imperative but can lead to better designs for all users. The constraints imposed by designing for accessibility often lead to clearer, simpler, and more robust interfaces. For example, ensuring keyboard navigability benefits power users; using clear language benefits everyone, not just EAL/D learners; providing alternatives to colour benefits those with colour blindness but also improves clarity in monochrome printing or projection. These characteristics also tend to reduce extraneous cognitive load, making the tool more usable and effective for the entire spectrum of learners.

## **6\. Synthesis and Evidence Appraisal**

**6.1 Synthesis of Findings**

This review synthesised empirical evidence to identify design principles for Tufte-inspired IDVs aimed at improving NSW Stage 5 students' data science learning outcomes (data literacy, modelling, Working Scientifically \- SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-05/06/07) within AI-supported classrooms, while managing cognitive load. The findings indicate that realising the potential of IDVs requires a multi-faceted approach, integrating considerations from cognitive science, visual design, interaction design, pedagogy, and practical implementation.

Effective IDVs for this context should prioritise **cognitive load management**. Given the limited working memory capacity of early adolescents, designs must minimise extraneous load by physically integrating related information, using signalling techniques to guide attention, and employing scaffolding such as progressive disclosure and learner-paced segmentation for dynamic content. Interactivity itself adds load, making such scaffolding essential, not optional.

**Visual design principles**, including those inspired by Tufte, offer valuable guidance but require adaptation. While clarity is paramount, strict minimalism may need tempering for novice learners, potentially incorporating task-relevant cues or familiar metaphors to aid comprehension and engagement. Small multiples are effective for comparison, and focus+context techniques aid exploration of large datasets. Effective layering and separation are crucial for managing visual complexity.

**Interaction design** should leverage intuitive patterns like direct manipulation and linked multi-views to facilitate exploration and the discovery of relationships. Details-on-demand via tooltips helps manage clutter. Narrative structures can provide guidance but may limit inquiry. The choice depends on learning goals.

**AI-enhanced adaptivity** offers potential for personalisation, particularly through adaptive scaffolding based on learner models incorporating prior knowledge and interaction analysis. However, effectiveness hinges on accurate learner modelling, and implementation faces significant technical and cost barriers. AI may help dynamically balance guidance and exploration.

Crucially, the **pedagogical integration** determines the ultimate impact. IDVs show benefits over static visuals primarily when tasks genuinely require the exploration and manipulation afforded by interactivity, and when accompanied by appropriate guidance. Formative assessment strategies like embedded questions and self-explanation prompts are vital, acting as both assessment tools and learning scaffolds. Assessing process skills requires new approaches, potentially using interaction logs.

Finally, **equity, accessibility, and scalability** must be central considerations. Designs need to adhere to accessibility standards, function across diverse devices and bandwidth conditions, and be supported by robust teacher professional learning, technical infrastructure, sustainable cost models, and clear curriculum alignment. There is an inherent tension between sophistication and equitable access that must be carefully managed.

**6.2 Evidence Strength Appraisal**

The strength of the empirical evidence supporting these findings varies across domains. A modified Kirschner/CESE (Centre for Evaluation and Monitoring, Durham University) rubric approach, considering study design (e.g., experimental, quasi-experimental, correlational), sample characteristics (size, relevance to target age group), ecological validity, effect sizes, and consistency across studies, was conceptually applied.

* **Strong Evidence:** There is strong, consistent evidence from cognitive science and multimedia learning research supporting principles for managing cognitive load, such as the negative impact of split-attention and the benefits of signalling and segmentation. The effectiveness of interaction techniques like linked views for identifying relationships is also well-supported in the information visualisation literature. The critical role of teacher professional learning for technology integration is robustly established.
* **Moderate Evidence:** Evidence for the benefits of specific scaffolding techniques like progressive disclosure in visual analytics tools is growing. The effectiveness of small multiples and focus+context techniques is generally accepted in HCI/InfoVis, though educational validation may be less extensive. The finding that IDV benefits are contingent on task design has moderate support from comparative studies. The utility of formative assessment techniques like self-explanation prompts is well-supported in learning sciences, with emerging application to IDVs. Accessibility guidelines are well-defined, though empirical studies on their impact within complex IDVs might be fewer.
* **Weaker/Emerging Evidence:** Empirical validation of specific Tufte principles (like data-ink ratio) within *interactive educational* contexts for *adolescents* is less conclusive, with some studies suggesting boundary conditions. Evidence for the effectiveness of *AI-driven adaptive IDVs* specifically in K-12 science settings is still emerging, often extrapolated from related domains, and practical implementation challenges are significant. Comparative studies directly measuring the impact of IDVs on specific NSW curriculum outcomes are likely limited, requiring inference based on measured skills (e.g., pattern identification). Longitudinal studies tracking the development of data science skills using IDVs over time are also scarce.

**Gaps in Research:** Further research is needed specifically targeting NSW Stage 5 students, using IDVs designed with Tufte-inspired principles and AI adaptivity, measuring impacts on validated data literacy, modelling, and Working Scientifically skill assessments. More studies comparing different scaffolding strategies within IDVs for this age group, investigating the nuances of Tufte's principles in interactive learning, and exploring cost-effective and scalable models for AI adaptivity and teacher PL are warranted. Research examining the interplay between IDV design, pedagogical approaches, and equity outcomes in authentic classroom settings is also crucial.

## **7\. Recommendations**

Based on the synthesis of empirical evidence, the following recommendations are offered for the design, selection, pedagogical integration, and implementation of interactive data visualisations (IDVs) to support NSW Stage 5 (Year 9-10) data science learning outcomes (SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-05/06/07) within potentially AI-supported classrooms:

**For Designers and Developers:**

1. **Prioritise Cognitive Load Management:**
   * Minimise split-attention by physically integrating labels, legends, and essential explanatory text directly within the visualisation.
   * Employ signalling techniques (e.g., highlighting, arrows) judiciously to guide attention to critical information, especially in complex displays.
   * Implement progressive disclosure for interface controls and data layers to reduce initial complexity.
   * Ensure dynamic or animated visualisations are segmented and learner-paced to prevent overload.
2. **Apply Visual Design Principles Thoughtfully:**
   * Strive for visual clarity and minimise distracting clutter, but evaluate minimalist designs (high data-ink) with target learners to ensure they do not hinder engagement or comprehension; consider task-relevant cues where beneficial.
   * Utilise small multiples for tasks requiring comparison across categories or conditions.
   * Employ effective layering and visual separation techniques to structure complex information.
   * Incorporate focus+context mechanisms (e.g., zooming, overview+detail) for exploring large datasets.
3. **Leverage Effective Interaction Patterns:**
   * Implement intuitive direct manipulation controls (e.g., drag-to-filter, brushing) with clear, immediate visual feedback.
   * Use linked multiple views to help students discover relationships between variables represented differently.
   * Provide details-on-demand (e.g., hover tooltips) to offer specific information without cluttering the main display.
   * Consider narrative structures or guided tours for introductory activities or complex topics, but ensure options for open-ended exploration are also available.
4. **Design for Accessibility and Equity:**
   * Adhere to WCAG standards for accessibility from the outset (keyboard navigation, screen reader compatibility, colour contrast, alternatives to colour).
   * Use clear, simple language and consider EAL/D learner needs.
   * Design for responsiveness across various screen sizes (desktops, tablets, potentially mobiles).
   * Optimise performance for potentially lower-spec devices and limited bandwidth conditions; consider offline capabilities.
5. **Approach AI Adaptivity Strategically:**
   * If implementing AI, focus initially on adaptive scaffolding (e.g., tailored hints, feedback) based on robust learner model inputs like prior knowledge and task performance.
   * Explore AI for analysing interaction logs to provide formative assessment insights to teachers.
   * Ensure AI algorithms and decision-making processes are transparent and understandable to educators.
   * Carefully consider the cost, technical complexity, and data privacy implications.

**For Educators and Curriculum Developers:**

6. **Integrate IDVs Purposefully:**
   * Select or design learning tasks that genuinely leverage the interactive capabilities of the IDV for exploration, data manipulation, comparison, or hypothesis testing – tasks difficult to achieve with static visuals.
   * Clearly articulate the learning objectives and how using the IDV helps achieve them, explicitly linking to NSW syllabus outcomes (SC4-DA1-01, SC4-WS-05/06/07).
   * Provide explicit instruction and guidance on how to use the IDV's features effectively for data analysis, especially during initial encounters.
7. **Embed Formative Assessment:**
   * Integrate embedded questions within IDV activities to check comprehension and guide attention.
   * Use self-explanation prompts to encourage students to articulate their reasoning and interpretations of the data.
   * Observe student interaction and use think-alouds (perhaps in pairs) to gain insights into their sense-making processes.
   * Utilise data from interaction logs (if available) to identify common difficulties or misconceptions and inform instruction.
8. **Develop Process-Oriented Assessment:**
   * Complement traditional assessments with methods that evaluate students' data inquiry processes (e.g., assessing the quality of self-explanations, evaluating analysis strategies reflected in interaction patterns or student reports).

**For Policymakers and School Leaders:**

9. **Invest in Sustained Teacher Professional Learning:**
   * Provide ongoing, high-quality PL that focuses not just on technical skills but on pedagogical strategies for using IDVs effectively to teach data science concepts and Working Scientifically skills, including formative assessment techniques.
10. **Ensure Equitable Access:**
    * Consider equity implications when selecting or procuring IDV tools, prioritising solutions that are accessible, function across a range of school devices, and are usable under varying bandwidth conditions.
    * Address the digital divide through appropriate device provision and infrastructure support where needed.
11. **Plan for Sustainability:**
    * Evaluate the total cost of ownership (licensing, hardware, support, training) and select tools with sustainable models.
    * Ensure adequate technical support and robust IT infrastructure are in place to support the use of IDVs.
12. **Promote Curriculum Alignment:**
    * Support the development or identification of high-quality IDV resources and associated learning activities that are clearly aligned with NSW curriculum requirements.

By addressing these interconnected factors – thoughtful design, purposeful pedagogy, and supportive implementation structures – interactive data visualisations, potentially enhanced by AI, can become powerful tools for developing essential data science competencies in NSW Stage 5 students.

## Abstract

Motivation and instruction are two major substantive domains in educational psychology. Theory and research relevant to each of these domains tend to be diffuse and fragmented. This presents challenges for scholars and practitioners seeking to implement parsimonious and cohesive approaches to help students to learn. This review articulates a two-step integration process that is an illustrative effort towards tackling these challenges and unifying two psycho-educational domains: intra-domain integration (within each of motivation and instruction) and inter-domain integration (between motivation and instruction). With respect to motivation, the Motivation and Engagement Wheel (Martin, 2007) is presented as an example of intra-domain integration of key facets of motivation (Step 1a). With respect to instruction, Load Reduction Instruction (LRI; Martin, 2016; Martin & Evans, 2018) is an example of intra-domain integration of explicit instruction and guided independent learning (Step 1b). The review then proposes an inter-domain integration of motivation and instruction (Step 2), arguing that each domain is tied to the other. The joint operation of intra- and inter-domain integration of motivation and instruction holds potential for more coherent theorizing, measurement, and practical application.

## The Motivation and Engagement Wheel

There are four higher order dimensions and eleven lower order factors in the Motivation and Engagement Wheel. The four higher order dimensions are: positive motivation, positive engagement, negative motivation, and negative engagement. Positive motivation is a predominantly cognitive-affective dimension and comprises a set of convergent factors that reflect students’ positive attitudes and orientations to academic learning, including:
1. Self-efficacy
2. Valuing
3. Mastery orientation

Positive engagement is a predominantly behavioral dimension and comprises a set of convergent factors that reflect students’ positive behaviors and engagement in academic learning, including:
4. Planning and monitoring behavior
5. Task management
6. Persistence

Negative motivation is a predominantly maladaptive cognitive-affective dimension and comprises a set of convergent factors that reflect students’ attitudes and orientations inhibiting academic learning, including:
7. Anxiety
8. Failure avoidance
9. Uncertain control

Negative engagement is a predominantly maladaptive behavioral dimension and comprises two convergent factors that reflect students’ more problematic learning behaviors, including:
10. Self-handicapping
11. Disengagement
